# Mosquito Bay

![Mosquito Bay, Vieques](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/82/Mosquito_bay%2C_Bioluminescent_bay%2C_Vieques_-_panoramio.jpg/1280px-Mosquito_bay%2C_Bioluminescent_bay%2C_Vieques_-_panoramio.jpg)
*Mosquito Bay by day. Photo: Edgar Torres, CC BY 3.0*

**Bahía Mosquito, on the south coast of [[vieques|Vieques]]. Officially the brightest
bioluminescent bay in the world**, and the single best reason to build the trip around a
particular weekend.

## Why it's the brightest

Guinness recognised it as the brightest bioluminescent bay in the world in **2008**, and
it holds that on numbers: an estimated **700,000 dinoflagellates per gallon** of water.

![Bioluminescent dinoflagellates](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/08/Dinoflagellate_bioluminescence.jpg)
*Dinoflagellates lighting up when disturbed. Photo: Jed, CC BY-SA 2.0*

The organism is **_Pyrodinium bahamense_**, a single-celled dinoflagellate that emits a
blue flash when the water around it is agitated. Every paddle stroke, every ripple, every
hand dragged through the water lights up.

![Pyrodinium under the microscope](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/65/CSIRO_ScienceImage_7608_Pyrodinium.jpg)
*Pyrodinium. Photo: CSIRO, CC BY 3.0*

Two bits of geography make this bay exceptional rather than merely good:

1. **It's shallow and the mouth is narrow.** The organisms get trapped and concentrated
   instead of flushing out to sea.
2. **Mangroves ring the bay.** They shed the nutrients *Pyrodinium* feeds on, so the
   population is continually fed.

![Vieques mangroves](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8b/Mangroves_in_Vieques%2C_Puerto_Rico_after_Hurricane_Maria_2017.jpg/1280px-Mangroves_in_Vieques%2C_Puerto_Rico_after_Hurricane_Maria_2017.jpg)
*The mangroves that feed the bay. Photo: Western Area Power, CC BY 2.0*

## 🌑 The moon decides everything

This is the whole planning problem. **The glow competes with ambient light, so a bright
moon washes it out.** Tours run regardless and operators will happily sell you a ticket
on a full moon; you'll just see far less.

Saturday-night moon illumination for the weekends in play:

| Weekend | Moon | |
|---|---|---|
| Jan 8–11 | 4% | ★ excellent |
| **Jan 15–18 (MLK)** | **66%** | poor |
| Feb 5–8 | **0%** | ★ perfect |
| **Feb 12–15 (Presidents')** | **50%** | workable |
| Mar 5–8 | 2% | ★ excellent |

Full analysis and the trade-off against keeping a holiday Monday is on the
[[index|trip dashboard]].

## 🚫 You cannot swim in it

Swimming is **prohibited** by Puerto Rico's Department of Natural Resources, and it's
enforced. This isn't liability caution, it's the reason the bay still works.

**Sunscreen, insect repellent and DEET kill the dinoflagellates.** Enough swimmers over
enough years and the population collapses. Older accounts and photos of people swimming
in the glow are from before the rule, and that's exactly the behaviour that damaged it.

**Practical upshot:** go in clean. Don't put on sunscreen or bug spray before the tour.
Bring long sleeves instead if the mosquitoes bother you.

## Booking and logistics

- **Licensed operators only.** You cannot drive up and put a kayak in
- Tours stage from **Esperanza**, the main tourist town on the south coast
- **1 to 2 hours** on the water, kayak or canoe
- Book well ahead for a dark-moon date. Those nights sell out first, because everyone
  who's researched it wants the same nights you do

## What to actually expect

Set expectations before anyone books flights around this. **It is not the neon-blue
image you've seen online.** Long-exposure photography exaggerates it enormously. What
you get in person is a soft blue-green glow that appears where the water is disturbed and
fades a second or two later.

**Your phone will almost certainly not capture it.** Cameras that can are on tripods
doing multi-second exposures, which is not available to you in a moving kayak. Plan to
look at it rather than photograph it.

On a genuinely dark night it's still one of the strangest things you'll see.
